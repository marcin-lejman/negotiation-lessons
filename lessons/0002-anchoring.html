Lesson 0002 · Contract Negotiation · ~15 minutes

Anchoring: The First Number

The first number spoken bends every number that follows. Decide deliberately whether it's yours — and know exactly what to do when it's theirs.

Builds on Lesson 0001. Your deal sheet gave you an aspiration point and an estimated ZOPA. Anchoring is what those numbers are for at the table: the aspiration point becomes your anchor when you go first, and the ZOPA estimate tells you whether you should go first at all.

1. Why one number bends the whole deal

Anchoring is the bias Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated in the 1970s: under uncertainty, people give far too much weight to the first number introduced — and adjust insufficiently away from it — even when that number is arbitrary. In their classic study, people who first saw the number 10 guessed African UN membership at 25%; people who saw 65 guessed 45% (PON: What is Anchoring).

In a negotiation, the first offer "frames what feels reasonable" for everything after it: a high anchor pulls attention to the offer's positive qualities; a low one highlights its flaws. The supplier's opening quote isn't information about price — it's an attempt to define the playing field. You proved this to yourself in Lesson 0001's quiz: Coldline's $2,400 opener made $2,380 feel like progress, even though your sheet said the zone ran to $2,100.

2. Should you name the first number?

Old advice says never go first — "you'll show your cards." Research disagrees: negotiators who made the first offer consistently achieved better economic outcomes (Rosette, Kopelman & Abbott, via PON: The First-Offer Dilemma) — provided the anchor is credible. The deciding factor is who knows the ZOPA better:

GO FIRST when: • you know the market / the ZOPA well (your deal sheet is solid) • you can justify the number with data (quotes, benchmarks, history) • neither side knows the ZOPA well (your anchor sets the frame) LET THEM go first when: • they know the ZOPA much better than you (your anchor would misfire: too timid = money gone, too wild = credibility gone) • you genuinely lack market information (their number IS information — but treat it as a move, not truth)

Notice what this means for you specifically: in recurring supplier relationships, where you hold purchase history, competing quotes, and volume data, the conditions for going first are usually met. Your habit of waiting for the supplier's quote — standard procurement reflex — hands them the frame in exactly the situations where research says you should be setting it.

Two findings sharpen the move:

Ambitious but defensible. The effective anchor is not an extreme bluff — it's your aspiration point, tied to objective standards: "preparation — not bravado — is what makes anchoring effective" (PON). An anchor you can't justify when challenged collapses, and takes your credibility with it. This is the same defensibility test from your deal sheet's aspiration line.

Precise beats round. Precise numbers anchor harder than round ones — research found a house listed at $255,500 attracts higher offers than at $255,000 (PON: Strive for Precision). A precise figure signals you computed it from something; a round one signals you made it up. Open at $2,185, not $2,200.

The comfort trap. The same research found first-movers got better outcomes but felt worse — more anxiety, lower satisfaction. Going first works and feels bad; expect that, and don't read your own discomfort as evidence the move was wrong. Rehearse the number out loud before the meeting so it leaves your mouth cleanly.

3. When the anchor is theirs: the three-move defense

You won't always go first — and suppliers anchor you constantly ("list price is...", "our other customers pay..."). PON's countermeasures (source) compress to three moves, in order:

  1. Name it dead before you counter. Defuse explicitly so your counter isn't read as bargaining within their range — never haggle from inside their frame. "I'm not trying to play games, but we're miles apart on price. Let me tell you where this actually needs to land."
  2. Ask for the basis. Make them justify the number against something objective. Either it has a basis (real information for you) or it doesn't (the anchor publicly deflates). "Help me understand how you got to that figure — what's it built on?"
  3. Counter with your justified number. Re-anchor at your aspiration point with its rationale attached — fair, precise, defensible. "Based on our volume this year and the two competing quotes on my desk, $2,185 is where this pencils out."

What not to do: respond to a wild anchor with a small counter-adjustment ("$2,400? How about $2,350?"). That validates the anchor and concedes the frame — the single most common anchoring loss in procurement.

4. Check yourself

New scenario, same supplier world. Feedback is instant.

You're renegotiating your annual pricing agreement with Coldline — your fourth year with them. You have purchase history, two fresh competing quotes, and a solid deal sheet. The meeting opens with small talk; pricing is about to come up. Who should name the first number?

Right. This is the textbook go-first situation: strong market knowledge, justifiable number, recurring relationship. Waiting for their quote — the standard procurement reflex — hands them the frame for no informational gain you don't already have. And remember the comfort trap: going first will feel slightly wrong even when it's right.
Check the decision rule: let them go first only when they know the ZOPA much better than you, or you lack market data. Neither applies — you have history, quotes, and a deal sheet. Their opening quote adds almost no information you don't have, but it would cost you the frame. (And "no one anchors" isn't a real option: the first number will be spoken by someone; the only question is whose.)

Your deal sheet says aspiration $2,200, reservation $2,390. You've decided to go first. Which opening is strongest?

Yes — all three findings in one number. It sits at your ambitious-but-defensible aspiration point, it's precise (signals computation, anchors harder than round $2,200), and the justification is pre-attached so the anchor survives the inevitable "how did you get there?" challenge. $1,900 fails the defensibility test: when you can't back it, it collapses and damages both credibility and a relationship you need for years.
Two tests for an opening anchor: defensible (can you justify it when challenged? $1,900 fails — it collapses and costs credibility with a long-term supplier) and precise (research shows $2,185 anchors harder than a round $2,200, because precision signals the number was computed, not invented). The strongest opening passes both and arrives with its rationale already attached.

A new supplier you don't know well opens with "$3,100 per unit — that's our standard project rate." Your sheet says the ZOPA likely tops out near $2,400. Your best next move?

Exactly the three-move sequence: kill the anchor explicitly first ("a long way apart"), then make them justify it. Either their basis teaches you something real about their costs, or the $3,100 publicly deflates. Only then do you re-anchor with your justified number. Countering at $2,950 would be the classic loss — it accepts their frame and makes the midpoint ~$2,700, far above your entire ZOPA.
Countering near their number validates the anchor — you'd be negotiating inside their frame, with a midpoint around $2,700 when your zone tops out at $2,400. And counter-anchoring instantly (option c) without defusing first reads as haggling: they'll average the two numbers. Sequence matters: defuse explicitly, ask the basis, then place your justified counter.

After you anchor at $2,185, the Coldline rep laughs: "Come on, you know we can't do that." No counter-number, just pushback. What's happening, and what do you do?

Right. Pushback without a counter-offer is a test of whether your anchor is real, not a rejection. You built the number to be defensible — so defend it: restate the volume data and quotes, then stop talking. Moving your own number before they've countered would be bidding against yourself — and it teaches a recurring supplier that your anchors melt under one laugh, which repricing every future deal.
Read the move: they gave you pressure, not a counter-offer. Conceding now (or withdrawing the anchor) means bidding against yourself — and with a recurring supplier it sets a precedent that your numbers move under social pressure alone, which you'll pay for in every future negotiation. Your anchor was built defensible for exactly this moment: restate its basis calmly, then let silence do the work.

5. Your win: anchor a real deal

For your next negotiation (or rerun a recent one):

  1. Fill in the deal sheet first — anchoring without it is bravado.
  2. Apply the decision rule: who knows the ZOPA better? Write down go first or let them, and why.
  3. If going first: write your opening as a precise number + attached justification, one sentence. Rehearse it out loud twice — the comfort trap is real.
  4. If they anchor first: write your defusing line and your basis-probing question in advance, so move one is ready before you're in the room.

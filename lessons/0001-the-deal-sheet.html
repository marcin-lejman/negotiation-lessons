Lesson 0001 · Contract Negotiation · ~15 minutes

The Deal Sheet

One page, three numbers, prepared before every negotiation. This is the structure your instincts have been missing.

Why this first? You negotiate supplier and distribution deals constantly, and you're good at the table. But Harvard's Program on Negotiation calls failure to prepare the biggest mistake negotiators make — unprepared negotiators "leave value on the table and get taken advantage of" (PON preparation checklist). Their full checklist runs 32 questions; the deal sheet is the five that move price and terms.

1. The chain: three numbers, in order

Every deal sheet is built in a fixed order, because each number derives from the one before it:

1. BATNA what will I actually do if there's no deal? 2. Reservation point that alternative, priced on THIS deal's terms 3. Aspiration point the ambitious-but-defensible target 4. Their side my best estimate of THEIR BATNA + reservation 5. ZOPA the overlap — where the deal will land

BATNA — your Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — is what you will really do if talks fail: the second-best supplier, buying a smaller quantity elsewhere, postponing the purchase. It must be real and available, not hypothetical (PON on BATNA). Your power in the room is exactly as strong as this alternative — which is why step one of preparation is often improving it: get a second quote before the meeting, not after.

Reservation point is your BATNA translated into a walk-away number on this deal: the price and terms at which signing and walking feel identical. Worse than this, you walk — calmly, because you know exactly what you're walking to.

Aspiration point is the "ambitious, but not outrageous, goal" (PON checklist, item 11). Negotiators who set explicit optimistic targets consistently get more than those who only know their floor — because without a target, your reservation point quietly becomes your goal, and you "win" by barely not losing.

ZOPA — the Zone of Possible Agreement — is the overlap between your reservation point and theirs (PON on ZOPA). Every signable deal lives inside it. Technique decides where inside it you land; preparation tells you how big it is.

The equivalence trap. PON's core warning when setting your reservation point: compare alternatives on truly equivalent terms. A competing supplier quoting 30% less is not actually cheaper if the warranty is shorter, lead time longer, or payment terms harsher (PON: "a BATNA in the bush"). For your recurring suppliers this matters doubly: the incumbent's reliability is part of the price comparison.

2. Worked example

Scenario · used in the quiz below

You're buying 40 commercial refrigeration units for a project. Your incumbent supplier, Coldline, quotes $2,400/unit, 60-day delivery, 2-year warranty, 30-day payment terms. You request a competing quote from FrostTech, a supplier you've used once: $2,250/unit, but 90-day delivery and 1-year warranty. The project can absorb 90-day delivery at a real cost of roughly $80/unit in schedule risk, and you value Coldline's extra warranty year at about $60/unit.

Deal sheet lineValueReasoning
BATNAFrostTech @ $2,250Real, quoted, available
Reservation point$2,390/unit$2,250 + $80 schedule risk + $60 warranty = FrostTech priced on equivalent terms
Aspiration point$2,200/unitDefensible: 40-unit volume + visible competing quote justify asking below both quotes
Their side (est.)~$2,100 floorColdline won't want to lose a recurring account over one project; margin likely allows it
ZOPA$2,100 – $2,390Wide zone. Preparation just told you $2,400 is outside it.

Note what happened: Coldline's $2,400 quote felt fine until the sheet showed it's above your reservation point. And FrostTech's "$150 cheaper" quote shrank to $90 once priced on equivalent terms. Two instinct-level errors caught on one page.

3. Check yourself

Same scenario. Answer all four — feedback is instant.

Mid-meeting, Coldline's rep says: "I can do $2,380, final offer." What does your deal sheet tell you?

Right. $2,380 < $2,390 reservation → signable. But the sheet exists precisely so "signable" doesn't become "settled for": your target is $2,200 and the estimated ZOPA runs to $2,100. The reservation point tells you when to walk; the aspiration point tells you when to stop pushing. Improvement over the original quote appears in neither.
Look at the two boundary numbers: $2,380 is below your $2,390 reservation point (signable — so don't walk) but far above your $2,200 aspiration (so don't accept yet either). Progress vs. their opening quote is irrelevant — that's their anchor, not your sheet.

A colleague says: "FrostTech is $150 cheaper — just tell Coldline to beat $2,250 or lose the order." What's wrong with that?

Exactly — the equivalence trap. Your real walk-away is $2,390, not $2,250. If Coldline knows their delivery and warranty edge (good reps do), they may counter at $2,300 and call the bluff — and now you either overpay at FrostTech or visibly back down. Pressure is fine; pressure anchored to a number you can't actually walk to is not.
The issue isn't aggression or honesty — referencing a competing quote is normal practice. The issue is that $2,250 isn't an equivalent offer: add $80 schedule risk and $60 warranty value and FrostTech really costs $2,390. Threatening to walk to a number that isn't your true alternative is a bluff that a good rep can call.

Before the meeting you could spend two hours getting a third quote from a supplier likely to land near $2,300 with comparable terms. Worth it?

Yes. PON stresses that BATNA work is ongoing, and the single most powerful preparation move is improving your alternative before the meeting. A comparable-terms quote near $2,300 beats FrostTech's equivalent-adjusted $2,390, dropping your reservation point ~$90 — every dollar of which is now winnable at the table. Away-from-the-table moves often beat at-the-table technique.
Compare on equivalent terms: FrostTech's real cost to you is $2,390, so a comparable-terms quote at ~$2,300 would become your new best alternative and cut your reservation point by ~$90. Two hours to structurally strengthen your position in every exchange that follows is excellent value.

Why does the sheet force you to write the aspiration point ($2,200) and not just the walk-away ($2,390)?

This is the core insight for a practiced-but-unstructured negotiator. Knowing only your floor, anything above it feels like a win — so you drift to it. An explicit, defensible target re-aims the whole negotiation: research consistently shows negotiators with ambitious specific targets outperform those without (PON checklist). Walk-away protects you from bad deals; aspiration gets you good ones.
Not quite — whether you reveal the target is a separate tactical choice (that's anchoring, next lesson), and it's certainly not for reporting. The target's job is psychological and pre-emptive: without it, your floor silently becomes your goal, and you'll settle at $2,370 feeling good. The aspiration point is what makes you keep pushing inside the safe zone.

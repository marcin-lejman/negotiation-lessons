Lesson 0003 · Contract Negotiation · ~15 minutes

Concession Strategy

The anchors are set and the gap is open. How you close it decides both what you pay and what the relationship looks like afterward.

Builds on Lessons 0001–0002. Your deal sheet bounds the game; your anchor opens it. Everything between the opening numbers and the handshake is concessions — and this is the lesson where "best price" and "long-term relationship" stop competing: done right, every concession extracts value and builds goodwill at the same time.

1. The one rule

Unplanned negotiators treat concessions as lubricant — give a bit, keep things friendly, meet in the middle. Research says concessions are moves, and wasted unless the other side registers them. The whole discipline compresses to one rule:

NEVER FREE. Every concession is labeled, traded, and shrinking. labeled → they know it cost you something traded → it buys a defined concession back shrinking → each move is smaller than the last, signalling you're approaching your limit

The framework is Harvard Business School professor Deepak Malhotra's four strategies (PON: Four Strategies for Making Concessions). Two protect your value; two — strikingly — are about building the relationship while you do it.

2. The four strategies

① Label your concessions

Counterparts are motivated to overlook your concessions — an unnoticed concession creates no obligation to reciprocate. So make each one salient: say what it costs you, and frame the benefit to them. And move slowly off your opening — if you abandon your anchor instantly, leaving it registers as nothing (which is also why Lesson 0002 said the anchor must be defensible, not frivolous).

"Moving on price isn't easy for us — this comes straight out of the project margin. But it puts you well below what anyone else is quoting you'd match."

② Demand — and define — reciprocity

Labeling triggers the social norm of reciprocity; defining tells them how to pay. This is the step most negotiators skip, and the cost of skipping it is subtle: a counterpart left to choose will reciprocate with whatever is cheapest for them, not what's valuable to you.

"We've made a real adjustment on price. That should make it easier on your side to move the delivery up — hitting week 6 instead of week 9 would help us immeasurably."

③ Contingent concessions

When goodwill is met with silence, link the concession explicitly: it only exists if the named return-concession does. Malhotra calls these "nearly risk-free" — you signal flexibility while making exploitation impossible.

"We can take the extended warranty into the price — but only if the payment terms move to net 60."

④ Concessions in installments

Tversky and Kahneman again: people prefer good news in pieces. Two concessions of $30k then $10k land better than one of $40k — and the shrinking sequence (45 → 25 → 11, never 10 → 20 → 40) tells a story: the well is running dry. A growing sequence tells the opposite story and invites them to keep digging. Bonus: conceding in installments means you may shake hands before spending your full budget — and each installment is one more goodwill moment with a recurring supplier.

The contingency trap. Malhotra's warning, and it's aimed at relationships like yours: if every concession arrives with strings attached, you read as self-serving rather than cooperative, and trust erodes (PON). With long-term suppliers, default to label-and-define (①+②); save explicit contingency (③) for low trust, one-shot deals, or counterparts who pocketed a labeled concession without responding. And reciprocate their labeled concessions visibly — acknowledging their moves is what compounds into the relationship capital you're optimizing for.

3. Worked example: the concession ladder

Scenario · Coldline annual renewal, continued from Lesson 0002

Deal sheet: aspiration $2,185 (your anchor, already placed), reservation $2,390. Coldline counters at $2,350. Before the meeting you planned a concession ladder — your installments, each with its label and its price:

MovePriceStepTraded for / label
Anchor$2,185—"Volume + competing quotes" (Lesson 0002)
1st concession$2,230+45Defined trade: delivery to week 6
2nd concession$2,255+25Labeled: "out of project margin" + net 60 payment
3rd concession$2,266+11"That's genuinely the end of the room."

Three things to notice. The steps shrink (45 → 25 → 11): by move three, Coldline can read that you're at the wall, so the $11 figure is more believable than a round "final" $2,275 would be. Every step buys something defined — delivery, payment terms — so even your concessions improved the deal. And the ladder ends at $2,266, deep below your $2,390 reservation: planned installments mean you rarely spend the whole budget. Compare the unplanned version: anchor $2,185, panic under pushback, jump to $2,300 "to show good faith" — one unlabeled, untraded move that spends $115 and buys nothing, then invites the next squeeze.

4. Check yourself

Feedback is instant, as always.

You drop your price by $40/unit to keep momentum. The supplier's rep nods, writes it down, and moves to the next topic. What just happened?

Exactly. An unlabeled concession is invisible — counterparts are motivated to overlook it precisely so they don't owe one back (Malhotra). Worse, with a recurring supplier you've now taught a lesson of your own: this buyer moves $40 under light pressure. The fix isn't to never concede — it's to make the same $40 arrive labeled ("this comes out of margin") with a defined return ("which should make week-6 delivery workable").
Reciprocity isn't automatic — counterparts deliberately let unlabeled concessions pass unremarked so no obligation forms (Malhotra calls this out explicitly). A concession that buys "momentum" buys nothing: it was never priced, never traded, and it quietly reprices you as someone who moves under pressure. Label it, define the return, or don't make it.

You say: "We've moved meaningfully on price — that wasn't easy for us. We'd expect some flexibility from your side too." The rep replies: "Absolutely — we'll throw in free delivery on the first order." Delivery is worth almost nothing to you; you wanted payment terms. What went wrong?

Right — this is the step most negotiators skip. Undirected reciprocity gets answered with whatever costs them least, not what's worth most to you. The fix is one added sentence: "…and what would genuinely help us is net 60 on payment." Specific, immediate, and now their token gesture is measured against a named ask. (Labeling was correct — without it you'd have gotten nothing at all.)
Labeling was right and reciprocity did arrive — the failure is that it arrived in their currency. Left undirected, counterparts reciprocate with what's cheapest for them (free delivery) rather than what you value (payment terms). Demand and define: name the exact concession you want in the same breath as the label.

Planning your ladder from a $2,185 anchor toward a $2,390 reservation, which sequence of price moves is best designed?

Yes. Shrinking installments (45 → 25 → 11) do three jobs: each one lands as separate good news (Tversky & Kahneman — people prefer gains in pieces), the narrowing telegraphs "approaching the wall" so your endpoint is believable, and each step is a fresh opportunity to trade and build goodwill. Growing steps tell the opposite story — flexibility increasing under pressure — which invites more pressure.
Growing steps (b) signal that pressure works better over time — the worst possible message. And the single jump (c) fails twice: counterparts expect rounds, so an instant full concession reads as "there's more hidden" and gets pushed anyway — and you've spent your entire $81 budget in one move with nothing traded for it. Same total, sliced shrinking and traded, beats it on every axis.

Coldline — your supplier of four years — asks for a price adjustment on an add-on order. You can absorb it. Per this lesson, your best move?

Right. This is the contingency trap in reverse: with a high-trust recurring counterpart, making every move contingent reads as self-serving and erodes exactly the relationship capital your mission optimizes for. But silent absorption (b) wastes the move entirely — unlabeled goodwill earns nothing. Label it generously, skip the strings, and the obligation forms anyway — collectable at the annual renewal.
Hard contingency (a) on a small ask, with four years of trust behind it, is Malhotra's exact warning: behavior that "appears self-serving rather than cooperative" corrodes long-term relationships. But silence (b) is just as wrong — an invisible favor creates no obligation. The middle path is the labeled free concession: name its cost warmly, attach no strings, and let the reciprocity norm do its work on a counterpart you'll negotiate with for years.

5. Your win: plan a concession ladder

Extend the deal sheet for your next negotiation with a concession plan — before the meeting, when you're calm:

  1. Budget: the gap between your anchor and your reservation point is your total concession budget. Decide how much of it you intend to spend.
  2. Slice it shrinking: 3–4 installments, each smaller than the last.
  3. Price each step: write the label (what it costs you) and the defined return (what it buys — payment terms, lead time, volume commitment, warranty) next to each installment. Non-price terms you anchored ambitiously in Lesson 0002 are your cheapest currency here.
  4. Pre-write your reciprocity line for the first concession — label + defined ask, one sentence, rehearsed.

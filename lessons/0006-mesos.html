Lesson 0006 · Contract Negotiation · ~15 minutes

Multi-Issue Offers (MESOs)

Stop making one offer. Make three you value equally — and let their choice tell you what they really want.

Builds on Lessons 0002 and 0004. A MESO — Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers — is the variable map turned into a weapon: three complete packages, each worth the same to you, presented at once. Each package anchors (Lesson 0002), the set reveals their priorities without them saying a word (Lesson 0004's probing, automated), and research shows users are seen as more flexible while claiming more value — your price-and-relationship mission in a single move (PON: The Benefits of Multiple Offers).

1. Why three offers beat one

Research by Medvec, Galinsky, Leonardelli and Claussen-Schulz — four empirical studies — found that negotiators presenting multiple equivalent simultaneous offers achieved better outcomes than those making a single packaged offer, without sacrificing the relationship or losing credibility (PON). Three mechanisms do the work:

  • Information without interrogation. Which package they gravitate toward — or how they compare them — maps their priorities. It's the Lesson 0004 interest probe, answered by behavior instead of words.
  • Flexibility perception. Counterparts rate MESO users as more flexible and accommodating — while the offers themselves stay aggressive. You get relationship credit for the format while the content claims value.
  • Triple anchor. Three packages bracket the conversation inside your frame; a counter must engage your structure rather than replace it with theirs.

PON's one caveat: the method is "used far too infrequently" because it demands real preparation. You're already doing the preparation — the deal sheet and variable map are the inputs.

2. Constructing a MESO: score, assemble, aim high

The construction recipe (PON: How to Use MESOs) is your variable map with numbers attached:

1. WEIGHT the issues price 50 · lead time 20 · warranty 15 · payment 15 (relative importance to YOU, totalling 100) 2. SCORE the options within each issue, rate the realistic range (week-6 delivery = 100 pts · week-9 = 40 pts) 3. ASSEMBLE 3 packages different shapes, SAME total score to you — never more than three at once 4. AIM ABOVE TARGET every package scores better than your aspiration point — the info you give away is paid for by the triple anchor

Rule 4 is the balance of the whole technique: a MESO reveals more about your interests than a single offer does, so the packages must all sit on the ambitious side of your target — the anchoring offsets the disclosure. And one thing stays secret: never reveal that the packages are equivalent to you. Present them as "different structures that could work"; the equivalence is your private scoring, not shared information.

3. Worked example: three ways to buy the same units

Scenario · Coldline renewal — instead of one anchor, three

Your weights: price 50, lead time 20, warranty 15, payment 15. Aspiration: $2,200-equivalent. Each package below scores ≈ the same to you — and slightly better than target:

A$2,160net 30Full year24 mo / wk 8Predictability is king — plan trades around volume
B$2,195net 15Half year24 mo / wk 8Cash flow is the pain — payment terms are your currency
C$2,180net 30Full year36 mo / wk 6Margin matters most — they'd rather give service than price

Presented together: "There are a few structures that could work for us next year — A, B, or C. Which direction feels most workable on your side?" Whatever happens next — they pick one, lean toward one, or push back on all three — you've learned their priority ranking without asking a single direct question, and the conversation is happening inside your three anchors. Note B's trick: a higher price than A, traded for fast payment — cheap for you if your cash position allows it, and exactly the kind of valuation-gap logroll the variable map exists to find.

The cherry-picker. A sharp rep will try: "Let's do A's price with B's payment terms and C's warranty." That combination scores far below your aspiration — it was never on offer. The defense is structural, not verbal: return to your scoring system and generate three fresh packages that incorporate what their cherry-picking just revealed (now you know exactly which elements they value) while restoring your total score (PON). Cherry-picking isn't a crisis — it's the richest single piece of priority information the technique produces, as long as you never price the combination they assembled.

4. Check yourself

You present packages A, B, C. The rep says: "Honestly? None of these work for us." Best next move?

Right — this is PON's prescribed move. "Which comes closest" is explicitly non-binding, so it's easy to answer — and the answer is the priority map you came for. Rejection of all three is a negotiating posture, not information failure; the comparative question converts the posture back into data. Improving all three (c) is Lesson 0003's cardinal sin in triplicate: three unlabeled, untraded concessions purchased by one "no."
Handing them the pen (a) surrenders the triple anchor and the frame — their package will be built from their scoring system, not yours. Sweetening all three (c) pays three concessions for one flat "no," untraded and unlabeled. The research-backed move costs nothing: ask which package comes closest, explicitly without commitment. It's easy for them to answer precisely because it concedes nothing — and it hands you their priority ranking.

A colleague preparing the meeting with you suggests: "Let's tell Coldline all three packages are worth the same to us — it shows good faith and they'll just pick the one that suits them." What's wrong?

Right. "A, B and C are all the same to us" sounds generous, but it publishes your exchange rates: they can derive that you'll swap $35/unit for net-15 payment, or $20 for a warranty year. With those rates they can construct the package that's cheapest for them at every margin. Present the packages as "structures that could work" — share the options freely (that's the flexible-format credit), keep the valuations private. Lesson 0004's line again: priorities yes, limits and rates no.
The packages genuinely should be equivalent to you — that's what makes you indifferent and therefore relaxed about their choice (b is wrong). But equivalence is private infrastructure, not a talking point: revealed, it discloses your internal trade-rates (what a payment-term day or warranty month is worth in dollars to you), letting a sharp counterpart optimize against your map. The collaborative signal comes from offering choice at all — not from publishing the scoring behind it.

The rep responds: "Great — we'll take A's $2,160 price, with B's half-year commitment and C's 36-month warranty." Per this lesson, what now?

Exactly. The cherry-picked combination scores far below anything you offered — accepting it (b) hands over the gap. But refusing rigidly (c) wastes the gift: they just told you precisely which elements they value (deep price, low commitment, long warranty). The structural defense is to regenerate — three fresh packages, each containing some of what they grabbed, each restored to your target score. You keep the frame, they keep feeling heard, and the negotiation converges on a real package instead of a fantasy one.
Accepting (b) means paying for a package you never priced — the cherry-picked combination is worth far less to you than any of the three you offered; that delta is pure leakage. But a flat "no mixing" (c) is needlessly rigid and burns the technique's relationship dividend. The move is regenerate: their cherry-pick is the best priority intelligence MESO produces — feed it back into the scoring system and present three new equivalent packages built around what they revealed.

Why should every package in the MESO score above your aspiration point, and why stop at three?

Right on both counts. A MESO is unusually revealing — three structured packages say a lot about what you care about — so PON's guidance is to counterbalance disclosure with anchoring: every package opens above target, leaving room for the concession ladder. And the three-package cap comes from the research, not convention: beyond three, counterparts disengage from comparison, and comparison is the engine that produces your priority information.
Cherry-picking protection (c) doesn't come from aggressive scoring — a cherry-picked combination falls below target regardless; the protection is regenerating packages. The real logic: a MESO discloses interest information (that's its cost), and aiming every package above aspiration is how you charge for the disclosure (that's the anchoring offset). The cap at three is empirical — beyond it, counterparts stop genuinely comparing, and the comparison is what generates your information.

5. Your win: turn your variable map into a MESO (weekend bundle, part 4)

Take the variable map from Lesson 0004's exercise (same real deal) and:

  1. Add weights — distribute 100 points across your variables by importance.
  2. Score the ranges — within each variable, rate the realistic options (best = 100).
  3. Assemble three packages with visibly different shapes (a price-led one, a terms-led one, a service-led one) that score within a few points of each other — all above your aspiration.
  4. Predict — write one line: which package do you expect them to choose, and what would each choice tell you?

Bring it back with the rest of the bundle. I'll check the two failure modes: packages that aren't really equivalent (one secretly dominates), and packages so similar the choice reveals nothing.

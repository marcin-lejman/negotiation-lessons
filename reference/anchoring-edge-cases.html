Anchoring Edge Cases

RFQs & sealed bids · "list price" · non-price terms

1 · RFQs & sealed bids: anchoring before and after the envelope

  • Compete vs. negotiate is the upstream choice. A study of 4,000+ construction contracts: fixed-price, spec-clear work went to auction/sealed bid; complex or quality-sensitive work was negotiated. Auctions compress price but "make lower-quality providers competitive" and repel top suppliers in busy markets (PON: Negotiation or an Auction?). Sealed-bid the commodities; negotiate anything where quality, partnership, or supplier input matters.
  • Your RFQ document is an anchor. Publishing a budget or target price anchors bidders up to it — never disclose it. What you should anchor in the document: spec, volumes, contract terms, and evaluation criteria, so bidders compete inside your frame.
  • After bids open, normal anchoring resumes. Most RFQs end in a shortlist negotiation (BAFO round). Received bids are now anchors on you — run the equivalence check before letting the lowest bid become your reference. In the BAFO round you can anchor explicitly: "to win this, you'll need to be at $X."

2 · When "list price" is the anchor

Rule: never negotiate discount-from-list. Change the baseline. List price is a manufactured reference point designed to make any discount feel like a gain (PON: The Anchoring Bias — Consumers, Beware, Price Anchoring 101).
  • Run the standard three-move defense, tuned for list price: defuse — "list isn't a meaningful starting point for an account our size"; probe — "who actually pays list?" (almost nobody, and the rep knows it); re-anchor on an objective baseline: last year's realized price, a competing quote, indexed input costs.
  • Track realized unit price, not discount %. "We got 35% off" is their frame; a deeper discount off an inflated list can still be a worse price. Year-over-year realized price is the only honest metric for recurring suppliers.
Discount theater: a supplier raising list 10% then offering 5% more discount has raised your price while improving your "deal." Always reconstruct the absolute number.

3 · Anchoring on non-price terms

  • Anything quantifiable anchors: payment days, lead-time weeks, warranty months, SLA penalty %, MOQ. Same Kahneman–Tversky machinery, and counterparties defend non-price anchors far less vigilantly than price ones.
  • Anchor the package, not just the price. If your opening is price-only, the supplier re-anchors every other variable ("fine on price — 50% advance, 90-day lead"). Open with the full term set: price, payment, lead time, warranty together. Prerequisite: your deal sheet needs aspiration/reservation per variable, not just for price.
  • Range offers work on terms. Research on "bolstering range offers" (Ames & Mason, via PON: Single Offer or a Range?): a range from your target to better-than-target (e.g. "24–36 month warranty") anchors at the high end while reading as more reasonable and less relationship-abrasive than a bare demand.
  • Ambitious non-price anchors are concession fuel. Terms you anchor high but value low become cheap things to trade later — the bridge to concession strategy (Lesson 0003).
